TASK

You are annotating the semantic content of justifications produced by language models for One Night Ultimate Werewolf (ONUW).

For each sentence, identify the TYPES OF INFORMATION that the justification actively reasons with.

The goal is to determine WHAT the stated reasoning is grounded in.

Do NOT classify:
- logical or deductive form;
- causal, conditional, or other discourse relations;
- whether the reasoning is correct;
- whether the justification reflects the model's internal reasoning.

These aspects are outside this annotation task.


INPUT

You will receive:

1. `vote`: the model's final vote;
2. `sentences`: the complete justification, already divided into sentences.

Sentence boundaries are fixed.

Do not merge, split, rewrite, or reorder sentences.

You may use the complete justification as context when interpreting a sentence, but annotate only information expressed in that sentence.


GENERAL ANNOTATION PRINCIPLE

Annotate the distinct TYPES OF INFORMATION that the sentence actively uses as part of its stated reasoning.

A sentence may receive multiple categories when genuinely different semantic components are involved.

Information may be used to:
- support or motivate a judgement;
- exclude a possibility;
- challenge information;
- compare alternatives;
- motivate a voting decision;
- establish that the available evidence is insufficient.

Do NOT annotate:
- filler;
- a bare repetition of the final vote;
- information mentioned only as background;
- a category merely because a related keyword appears;
- the conclusion itself merely because its wording resembles one of the categories.

IMPORTANT:

A TOPIC MENTION IS NOT SUFFICIENT FOR AN ANNOTATION.

For example:

"multiple role swaps"
→ NOT automatically Mechanical

"many role claims"
→ NOT automatically Testimony

"conflicting claims"
→ NOT automatically ClaimComparison

A category should be assigned only when an actual proposition of that type is expressed and actively used in the reasoning.

For every annotation, provide the shortest exact span from the sentence that demonstrates the category.


CATEGORIES


1. MECHANICAL

Assign `Mechanical` only when the sentence explicitly states a CONCRETE ONUW rule, mechanical constraint, or rule-governed consequence concerning roles, actions, information, timing, card ownership, or the resulting game state.

This includes:
- what a specific role can or cannot do;
- night-action order;
- how a role action changes card ownership;
- information mechanically available to a role;
- constraints on possible role or card configurations;
- concrete consequences produced by ONUW role mechanics.

Examples:

"The Tanner does not wake during the night."
→ Mechanical

"The Troublemaker acts after the Robber."
→ Mechanical

"If Elliot robbed Ashley, Elliot would now hold Ashley's card."
→ Mechanical

"If Justin's card was swapped with Caitlynn's, Caitlynn would now hold Justin's original card."
→ Mechanical


Do NOT assign Mechanical merely because the sentence mentions:
- a role;
- a role swap;
- a role change;
- a night action;
- a card;
- the center;
- a possible game state.

Examples:

"There were multiple role swaps."
→ NOT Mechanical

"The discussion involved several role changes."
→ NOT Mechanical

"James was involved in the Robber action."
→ NOT Mechanical

"The Werewolf may currently be in the center."
→ NOT Mechanical

"All Werewolf cards could be in the center."
→ NOT Mechanical

These describe events, topics, possibilities, or hypothesized game states, but they do not state or apply a concrete game rule.

Use this diagnostic question:

WHAT SPECIFIC ONUW RULE OR MECHANICAL CONSEQUENCE IS EXPRESSED?

If no concrete rule or rule-governed consequence can be identified, do not assign `Mechanical`.

Victory conditions and consequences for winning or losing belong to `Payoff`, not `Mechanical`.


2. TESTIMONY

Assign `Testimony` when the sentence uses the content of a CONCRETE FACTUAL CLAIM attributed to a player.

This includes claims about:
- the player's role;
- an action performed;
- a card seen;
- a swap;
- an observation;
- an admission;
- another factual aspect of the game.

Examples:

"Ashley says she started as the Werewolf."
→ Testimony

"Dan says he swapped Justin and Caitlynn."
→ Testimony

"Kaelan claimed to see the Werewolf."
→ Testimony

"Mitchell says he saw the Robber card in the center."
→ Testimony


Do NOT assign Testimony merely because the sentence refers generically to:
- "claims";
- "role claims";
- "statements";
- "what players said";
- "conflicting accounts".

Examples:

"The discussion contains many role claims."
→ NOT Testimony

"There are several conflicting claims."
→ NOT Testimony

The content of a sufficiently specific factual claim must be expressed.


A player's accusation, suspicion, or evaluation of another player is NOT Testimony.

"Dan says Mike is the Werewolf."
→ SocialJudgment

"Dan says he saw Mike's Werewolf card."
→ Testimony

The first expresses Dan's judgement about Mike.
The second expresses a claimed factual observation.


3. SOCIALJUDGMENT

Assign `SocialJudgment` when another player's judgement ABOUT A PLAYER is actively used as information in the reasoning.

This includes:
- accusations;
- suspicions;
- judgements that a player is innocent or guilty;
- expressions of trust or distrust;
- voting preferences;
- endorsements of another player's suspicion;
- agreement or consensus about a player.

Examples:

"Dan directly accused Mike of being the Werewolf."
→ SocialJudgment

"Several players also suspect Ashley."
→ SocialJudgment

"Erin and Margaret both believe Clint is suspicious."
→ SocialJudgment

"Dan's accusation is the strongest lead against Mike."
→ SocialJudgment

"Eliminating Mike based on this direct accusation seems safest."
→ SocialJudgment


SocialJudgment concerns another player's evaluation of a PLAYER.

A generic judgement about the overall game state is not SocialJudgment.

"Mitchell thinks a Werewolf is probably present."
→ NOT SocialJudgment


Distinguish the content of an accusation from the behaviour of making that accusation:

"Mitchell suspects James, which adds to the case against James."
→ SocialJudgment

"Mitchell keeps redirecting the vote toward James, which makes Mitchell look suspicious."
→ Behavioral

In the second example, Mitchell's behaviour is being evaluated as evidence about Mitchell himself.


4. BEHAVIORAL

Assign `Behavioral` when the sentence reasons from a player's behaviour, credibility, manner, motive, incentive, or apparent strategy.

This includes:
- evasiveness;
- vagueness;
- defensiveness;
- apparent honesty or dishonesty;
- attempts to manipulate the discussion;
- attempts to redirect suspicion;
- strategic conversational behaviour;
- motives for lying or protecting another player;
- behaviour interpreted as characteristic of a role.

Examples:

"Clint is evasive about what happened during the night."
→ Behavioral

"Mitchell keeps redirecting suspicion toward James."
→ Behavioral

"James keeps asking everyone to vote for him, which looks like Tanner behavior."
→ Behavioral

"He would have a motive to protect the Werewolf."
→ Behavioral


Do NOT assign Behavioral simply because another kind of information leads to the conclusion that a player is lying.

"The Tanner does not wake during the night, so Julie's claim that she woke must be false."
→ Mechanical + Testimony
→ NOT Behavioral

The claim is rejected using a game rule, not an observation of Julie's behaviour.


5. CLAIMCOMPARISON

Assign `ClaimComparison` only when the relationship between TWO OR MORE SUFFICIENTLY SPECIFIC PLAYER CLAIMS or accounts is itself used as information.

This includes:
- contradiction;
- inconsistency;
- corroboration;
- agreement;
- compatibility or incompatibility;
- a change in one player's account.

Examples:

"Julie first says she did nothing at night, but later says she woke and saw a card."
→ Testimony + ClaimComparison

"Ashley's account agrees with Elliot's description of the swap."
→ Testimony + ClaimComparison

"Ashley says Elliot robbed her, whereas Elliot says he robbed Julie; the two accounts cannot both be true."
→ Testimony + ClaimComparison


Do NOT assign ClaimComparison merely because a sentence says that claims are:
- conflicting;
- inconsistent;
- contradictory;
- confusing.

Examples:

"There are many conflicting claims."
→ Uncertainty

"Players are making inconsistent role claims."
→ Uncertainty

"The discussion is confusing because everyone is saying contradictory things."
→ Uncertainty

These sentences use disagreement only to establish that the available information is unclear or unresolved. They do not compare sufficiently specific claims.


Use this diagnostic question:

CAN YOU IDENTIFY THE SPECIFIC CLAIMS OR ACCOUNTS WHOSE RELATIONSHIP IS BEING USED?

If not, do not assign `ClaimComparison`.


Do NOT assign ClaimComparison when a player's claim conflicts with a GAME RULE rather than another player claim.

"Since the Tanner does not wake during the night, Julie's claim that she woke must be false."
→ Mechanical + Testimony
→ NOT ClaimComparison


6. PAYOFF

Assign `Payoff` when the sentence invokes:
- a victory or defeat condition;
- the consequence of a voting outcome for winning or losing;
- the desirability of a voting action because of its possible game outcome.

This includes both stating a relevant win condition and applying it to a voting decision.

Examples:

"Team Village wins if a Werewolf is eliminated."
→ Payoff

"If no player holds a Werewolf, Team Village wins if nobody is eliminated."
→ Payoff

"If James is the Tanner, voting him out would give him the win."
→ Payoff

"Eliminating Mike maximizes the chance of winning by removing a Werewolf."
→ Payoff


Payoff is distinct from Mechanical.

Mechanical concerns role and card mechanics that determine the GAME STATE.

Payoff concerns WINNING, LOSING, or the consequences of a VOTING OUTCOME.


A sentence may contain both when distinct information is present:

"The swap would leave Caitlynn holding Justin's Werewolf card, so eliminating Caitlynn would give Team Village the win."
→ Mechanical + Payoff


7. UNCERTAINTY

Assign `Uncertainty` when the absence, insufficiency, ambiguity, or unresolved nature of the available information is itself used as part of the reasoning.

This includes:
- lack of evidence;
- lack of confirmation;
- inability to identify a player confidently;
- multiple unresolved possibilities;
- ambiguity about the game state;
- unresolved disagreement among claims;
- absence of a clear candidate.

Examples:

"No player has been definitively identified as a Werewolf."
→ Uncertainty

"There is no concrete evidence that a Werewolf is present."
→ Uncertainty

"The possible role changes make the final identities impossible to determine."
→ Uncertainty

"The conflicting role claims leave no clear candidate."
→ Uncertainty


Do NOT assign other categories merely because the description of the uncertainty contains related vocabulary.

Example:

"The discussion is highly confusing because of multiple role swaps and conflicting claims."

→ Uncertainty only

NOT Mechanical merely because "role swaps" appears.
NOT Testimony merely because "claims" appears.
NOT ClaimComparison merely because "conflicting claims" appears.


Do NOT assign Uncertainty merely because words such as:
- probably;
- possibly;
- likely;
- may;
- perhaps

are present.

The uncertainty itself must function as information in the reasoning.


8. OTHER

Assign `Other` only when the sentence actively reasons from a type of information that does not fit any category above.

Provide a short description in `other_description`.

Other is a coverage category, NOT a default category.

Do NOT use Other for:
- filler;
- rhetorical wording;
- bare conclusions;
- repetition of the vote;
- sentences containing no relevant semantic basis.

In those cases return:

"annotations": []


MULTI-LABEL PRINCIPLE

Multiple labels are allowed only when the sentence genuinely uses multiple distinct semantic components.

Each label must correspond to a distinct piece or type of information.


Example 1:

"Since the Tanner does not wake during the night, Julie's claim that she woke must be false."

Mechanical:
"the Tanner does not wake during the night"

Testimony:
"Julie's claim that she woke"

NOT ClaimComparison, because the claim is evaluated against a game rule rather than another player claim.


Example 2:

"Julie first says she did nothing at night, but later claims she woke and saw a card."

Testimony:
"Julie first says she did nothing at night"
and/or
"later claims she woke and saw a card"

ClaimComparison:
"Julie first says she did nothing at night, but later claims she woke and saw a card"


Example 3:

"Several players suspect Ashley, and Ashley keeps avoiding questions."

SocialJudgment:
"Several players suspect Ashley"

Behavioral:
"Ashley keeps avoiding questions"


Example 4:

"Dan says he swapped Justin and Caitlynn, which would move Justin's card to Caitlynn."

Testimony:
"Dan says he swapped Justin and Caitlynn"

Mechanical:
"which would move Justin's card to Caitlynn"


Do NOT assign multiple labels merely because the same phrase could loosely fit multiple definitions.


IMPORTANT NEGATIVE EXAMPLE

Sentence:

"The discussion is highly confusing due to multiple role swaps and conflicting claims regarding who is the Robber, Insomniac, and Tanner."

Correct annotation:

Uncertainty:
"highly confusing"

Do NOT annotate:
- Mechanical for "multiple role swaps";
- Testimony for "claims regarding who is the Robber, Insomniac, and Tanner";
- ClaimComparison for "conflicting claims".

The sentence refers generically to these phenomena but does not state:
- a concrete ONUW rule or mechanical consequence;
- a concrete factual player claim;
- a relationship between sufficiently specific player claims.
