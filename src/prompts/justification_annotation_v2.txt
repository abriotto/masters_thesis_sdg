TASK

You are annotating the semantic content of justifications produced by language models after playing One Night Ultimate Werewolf (ONUW).

For each sentence, identify the TYPES OF INFORMATION that the justification actively reasons with.

The goal is to determine WHAT the stated reasoning is grounded in.

Do NOT classify:
- the logical form of the reasoning;
- causal, conditional, deductive, or other discourse relations;
- whether the reasoning is correct;
- whether the justification faithfully reflects the model's internal reasoning.

Those questions are outside this annotation task.

INPUT

You will receive:

1. `vote`: the model's final vote;
2. `sentences`: the complete justification, already divided into sentences.

Sentence boundaries are fixed.

Do not merge, split, rewrite, or reorder sentences.

You may use the full justification to interpret a sentence, but assign a category to a sentence only when the relevant information is expressed in that sentence.


GENERAL ANNOTATION PRINCIPLE

Annotate the distinct TYPES OF INFORMATION that the sentence actively uses as part of its stated reasoning.

A sentence may receive multiple categories when genuinely different types of information are involved.

Annotate information that is actively reasoned with, including information used to:
- support a judgement;
- exclude a possibility;
- challenge a claim;
- compare alternatives;
- motivate a voting decision;
- establish that the available evidence is insufficient.

Do not annotate:
- pure filler;
- a bare repetition of the final vote;
- information that is only mentioned as background and plays no role in the reasoning.

Do not create a category for the conclusion itself.

For every annotation, provide the shortest exact span of text that demonstrates that category.


CATEGORIES


1. MECHANICAL

Assign `Mechanical` when the sentence invokes information specific to the rules or mechanics of ONUW.

This includes:
- role abilities;
- night actions;
- night-action order;
- card swaps or card ownership;
- starting and ending roles;
- information available to particular roles;
- center-card constraints;
- mechanically possible or impossible game states;
- other consequences defined by the game rules.

Both stating a game mechanic and applying that mechanic to the current situation count as `Mechanical`.

Examples:

"The Tanner does not wake during the night."
→ Mechanical

"If Elliot swapped with Ashley, Elliot would now hold Ashley's card."
→ Mechanical

"The Troublemaker acts after the Robber."
→ Mechanical

Merely naming a role does NOT make a sentence Mechanical.

"Dan says he is the Troublemaker."
→ Testimony, not Mechanical

Merely reporting that a player claims to have performed an action also does not automatically make it Mechanical.

"Dan says he swapped Justin and Caitlynn."
→ Testimony

However, if the sentence invokes the mechanical consequence of the action:

"Dan says he swapped Justin and Caitlynn, which would move Justin's card to Caitlynn."
→ Testimony + Mechanical

`Mechanical` describes the semantic content being invoked. It does NOT mean that the reasoning is deductive or logically valid.


2. TESTIMONY

Assign `Testimony` when a factual assertion made by a player is actively used as information in the reasoning.

This includes claims about:
- a player's role;
- an action performed;
- a card seen;
- a swap;
- an observation;
- an admission;
- other factual information about the game.

Examples:

"Ashley says she started as the Werewolf."
→ Testimony

"Dan says he swapped Justin and Caitlynn."
→ Testimony

"Mitchell claims he saw the Robber card in the center."
→ Testimony

A player's opinion or suspicion about another player is NOT Testimony. It is `SocialJudgment`.

"Tyler says Clint is probably the Werewolf."
→ SocialJudgment


3. SOCIALJUDGMENT

Assign `SocialJudgment` when another player's judgement about a target is actively invoked in the reasoning.

This includes:
- accusations;
- suspicions;
- expressions of trust or distrust;
- voting preferences;
- endorsements;
- agreement with another player's suspicion;
- group consensus about a target.

Examples:

"Tyler also suspects Clint."
→ SocialJudgment

"Several players believe Ashley is the Werewolf."
→ SocialJudgment

"Erin and Margaret both agree that Clint is suspicious."
→ SocialJudgment

Distinguish the judgement itself from the behaviour of making that judgement.

"Mitchell suspects James, which adds to the case against James."
→ SocialJudgment

"Mitchell keeps trying to redirect the vote toward James, which makes Mitchell look suspicious."
→ Behavioral

In the second example, Mitchell's act of redirecting the vote is treated as evidence about Mitchell himself.


4. BEHAVIORAL

Assign `Behavioral` when the sentence reasons from a player's behaviour, credibility, manner, motives, incentives, or apparent strategy.

This includes:
- evasiveness;
- vagueness;
- defensiveness;
- apparent honesty or dishonesty;
- attempts to manipulate the discussion;
- attempts to redirect suspicion;
- motives for lying or protecting another player;
- behaviour interpreted as characteristic of a role.

Examples:

"Clint is evasive about what he did during the night."
→ Behavioral

"Mitchell keeps redirecting suspicion toward James."
→ Behavioral

"James keeps asking everyone to vote for him, which looks like Tanner behavior."
→ Behavioral

"As the Minion, he would have a motive to protect the Werewolf."
→ Behavioral

Do not assign Behavioral merely because another type of evidence leads to a conclusion about someone's honesty.

"The Tanner does not wake during the night, so Julie's claim that she woke must be false."
→ Mechanical + Testimony

The basis for rejecting Julie's claim is the game mechanic, not an observation of Julie's behaviour.


5. CLAIMCOMPARISON

Assign `ClaimComparison` when the relationship between two or more player claims is itself used as information.

This includes:
- contradiction;
- inconsistency;
- corroboration;
- agreement;
- compatibility or incompatibility;
- changes in a player's account.

Examples:

"Julie first says she did nothing at night, but later says she woke and saw a card."
→ Testimony + ClaimComparison

"Ashley's account agrees with Elliot's description of the swap."
→ Testimony + ClaimComparison

"Ashley says Elliot robbed her, while Elliot says he robbed Julie; the two accounts cannot both be true."
→ Testimony + ClaimComparison

`Testimony` identifies the player claims being used as information.

`ClaimComparison` additionally identifies that the relationship between those claims is itself informative.

Do NOT assign ClaimComparison when a claim conflicts with a game rule rather than with another player claim.

"Since the Tanner does not wake during the night, Julie's claim that she woke must be false."
→ Mechanical + Testimony
→ NOT ClaimComparison

Do NOT assign ClaimComparison when conflicting claims are invoked only to establish general uncertainty.

"There are many conflicting role claims, so it is impossible to determine what happened."
→ Uncertainty


6. PAYOFF

Assign `Payoff` when the sentence reasons about the consequences of a vote or action for winning or losing the game.

This includes:
- whether eliminating someone produces a Team Village win;
- avoiding elimination of the Tanner;
- what should happen when no Werewolf is present;
- choosing between voting options because of their possible outcomes.

Examples:

"If James is the Tanner, voting him out would give him the win."
→ Payoff

"Even if Sian is the Minion, Team Village still needs to eliminate a Werewolf."
→ Payoff

"If no Werewolf is in play, nobody should be eliminated."
→ Payoff

Payoff may co-occur with Mechanical.

"The swap would leave Caitlynn holding the Werewolf card, so eliminating Caitlynn would give Team Village the win."
→ Mechanical + Payoff

`Mechanical` concerns the game state or rules.

`Payoff` concerns the desirability or consequence of a voting action.


7. UNCERTAINTY

Assign `Uncertainty` when the absence, insufficiency, ambiguity, or unresolved nature of the available information is itself used as a consideration in the reasoning.

This includes:
- lack of evidence;
- lack of confirmation;
- multiple unresolved possibilities;
- inability to determine a player's role;
- ambiguity caused by possible swaps;
- unresolved conflict among accounts;
- absence of a clear candidate.

Examples:

"No player has been definitively identified as a Werewolf."
→ Uncertainty

"There is no clear evidence that a Werewolf is present."
→ Uncertainty

"There are too many possible swaps to determine the final roles."
→ Uncertainty

"The conflicting role claims leave no clear candidate."
→ Uncertainty

Do NOT assign Uncertainty simply because words such as "probably", "possibly", "likely", or "may" appear.

The uncertainty itself must function as part of the reasoning.


8. OTHER

Assign `Other` only when the sentence actively reasons from a type of information that does not fit any category above.

Provide a short description in `other_description`.

`Other` is a coverage category, not a default category.

Do NOT use Other for:
- filler;
- rhetorical wording;
- a bare conclusion;
- repetition of the vote;
- a sentence containing no relevant semantic information.

In those cases return an empty annotation list.


IMPORTANT MULTI-LABEL RULES

Use multiple labels only when multiple distinct semantic components are genuinely involved.

Example:

"Since the Tanner does not wake during the night, Julie's claim that she woke must be false."

→ Mechanical:
   "the Tanner does not wake during the night"

→ Testimony:
   "Julie's claim that she woke"

Do NOT add ClaimComparison because Julie's claim is being evaluated against a game mechanic, not against another player claim.


Example:

"Julie first says she did nothing at night, but later claims that she woke and saw a card."

→ Testimony:
   "Julie first says she did nothing at night"
   and/or
   "later claims that she woke and saw a card"

→ ClaimComparison:
   "Julie first says she did nothing at night, but later claims that she woke and saw a card"


Example:

"Several players suspect Ashley, and Ashley keeps avoiding questions."

→ SocialJudgment:
   "Several players suspect Ashley"

→ Behavioral:
   "Ashley keeps avoiding questions"


Do not assign two labels merely because two category definitions could loosely describe the same phrase. Each assigned category must capture a genuinely distinct semantic component.
